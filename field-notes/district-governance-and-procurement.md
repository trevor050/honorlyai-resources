# District governance and procurement for K-12 AI

> **Direct answer:** A district needs more than a tool list. It needs a repeatable way to define purpose, approve use cases, review privacy and security, preserve professional judgment, communicate with the public, and revisit decisions as products and law change.

This hub connects the most relevant HonorlyAI field notes with editable governance tools in this repository.

## 1. Begin with governance, not a permanent ban

Blocking a chatbot can reduce unmanaged use on school systems while leaders review risk. It cannot tell teachers what assistance is allowed, protect students using personal devices, establish an approval pathway, or create a fair response process.

Read [AI bans are not an AI policy](https://honorlyai.com/blog/ai-bans-are-not-school-ai-policy) for a six-part governance framework covering approved tools, assignment boundaries, student data, human oversight, academic integrity, and incidents.

## 2. Track state policy without pretending proposals are law

State policy can change quickly. Districts should distinguish enacted requirements, official guidance, proposed legislation, and optional implementation practices.

For a dated New Jersey analysis, read [What New Jersey school districts should prepare for on AI policy in 2026](https://honorlyai.com/blog/new-jersey-school-ai-policy-2026). The article identifies the reviewed date and describes Senate Bill 4469 as proposed legislation rather than enacted law as of that date.

Use the [New Jersey AI policy readiness checklist](../templates/new-jersey-ai-policy-readiness-checklist.md) to document:

- official source and legislative status;
- board policy ownership;
- public question and concern routes;
- equity, accessibility, and language access;
- employee oversight and professional judgment;
- annual review and public posting readiness.

## 3. Separate the legal and operational questions

FERPA, COPPA, state student-privacy law, contracts, security, accessibility, and instructional review overlap, but they do not answer the same question.

Read [FERPA, COPPA, and state privacy laws: who reviews what when schools buy AI?](https://honorlyai.com/blog/ferpa-coppa-state-privacy-school-ai) for a cross-functional responsibility map.

Use these repository tools to preserve evidence:

- [School AI privacy review checklist](../templates/school-ai-privacy-review-checklist.md)
- [AI data lifecycle worksheet](../templates/ai-data-lifecycle-worksheet.md)
- [District AI vendor evaluation checklist](../templates/district-ai-vendor-evaluation-checklist.md)
- [District AI procurement question bank](../templates/district-ai-procurement-question-bank.md)

A useful approval should name the users, grades, purpose, features, data types, settings, term, conditions, and next review date. Avoid generic labels such as “FERPA approved.”

## 4. Procure the full service, not the demo response

A model can answer a prompt well while the product fails on identity, teacher controls, retention, accessibility, support, change management, or contract exit.

Read [The school AI procurement questions vendors hope you do not skip](https://honorlyai.com/blog/school-ai-procurement-questions) for questions about:

- educational purpose and excluded uses;
- complete data flow and subprocessors;
- model training, evaluation, and product improvement;
- teacher configuration and evidence;
- security and incident response;
- accessibility documentation and testing;
- material product change;
- export, deletion, and termination.

The [District AI procurement question bank](../templates/district-ai-procurement-question-bank.md) converts those topics into a written vendor response and evidence request.

## Suggested district sequence

1. Inventory current AI-enabled products and uses.
2. Define temporary boundaries for unreviewed student-facing services.
3. Assign a cross-functional review team and decision owner.
4. Use the procurement question bank and privacy checklist.
5. Negotiate enforceable terms and document the approved scope.
6. Run a narrow pilot with pre-defined evidence and stop conditions.
7. Publish plain-language policy and family communication.
8. Review annually and after material product, legal, or incident changes.
