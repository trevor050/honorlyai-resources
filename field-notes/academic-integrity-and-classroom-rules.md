# Academic integrity and classroom AI rules

> **Direct answer:** Strong academic-integrity practice begins before submission. Teachers should state the permitted AI actions, collect proportionate process evidence, protect accommodations, and use a human review process. Automated detector scores or alerts may prompt questions, but they should not become automatic proof.

This hub connects four canonical HonorlyAI field notes with editable classroom and due-process resources.

## 1. Treat detector output as uncertainty, not authorship evidence

An AI text detector estimates patterns in a document. It does not observe who wrote each sentence, which tools were used, whether assistance was permitted, or how the work changed through revision.

Read [Why AI detectors are the wrong foundation for academic integrity](https://honorlyai.com/blog/ai-detectors-academic-integrity-schools) for the research and policy case against detector-first enforcement.

Use the [AI detector and academic-integrity due-process checklist](../templates/ai-detector-due-process-checklist.md) to require:

- a stated assignment rule;
- independent evidence beyond an automated score;
- student notice and opportunity to explain;
- review of drafts, sources, version history, and prior work;
- accommodation and language-access consideration;
- documented human findings and appeal.

## 2. Put the boundary on the assignment

A districtwide policy cannot determine the correct level of assistance for every lab, essay, coding task, presentation, or assessment. The teacher should translate the learning goal into concrete actions students can follow.

Read [How to write assignment-level AI directions students can actually follow](https://honorlyai.com/blog/assignment-level-ai-directions) for a seven-part statement covering purpose, allowed and prohibited actions, tools and data, disclosure, independent responsibility, and review.

Use the [Assignment AI-use statement builder](../templates/assignment-ai-use-statement-builder.md) to produce a copy-ready statement and student disclosure prompt.

## 3. Distinguish the stages of writing

Brainstorming, research support, outlining, drafting, feedback, editing, translation, and citation assistance affect student authorship differently. A single “AI allowed” label hides the actual intellectual work being assessed.

Read [AI in student writing: rules for brainstorming, drafting, feedback, and editing](https://honorlyai.com/blog/ai-rules-student-writing) for stage-specific examples.

## 4. Give teachers useful visibility without creating a transcript panopticon

Teachers may need aggregate misconceptions, assignment-rule events, student-requested help, and relevant conversation evidence. They do not need to read every ordinary student exchange or maintain permanent behavioral profiles.

Read [Teacher visibility without student surveillance: where the line belongs](https://honorlyai.com/blog/teacher-ai-visibility-without-surveillance) for a proportional-access framework.

Use the [Teacher visibility and AI tutor review worksheet](../templates/teacher-visibility-ai-tutor-review.md) to document:

- the educational purpose for each signal;
- who can see aggregate and individual information;
- what justifies individual drill-down;
- retention and deletion;
- alert accuracy and subgroup review;
- student and family notice;
- the human action connected to each metric.

## A practical integrity workflow

1. Identify the skill the assignment must reveal.
2. Select the appropriate AI-use level.
3. Write permitted and prohibited actions in plain language.
4. Identify approved tools, accounts, and data limits.
5. Collect only the process evidence needed for the task.
6. Teach disclosure and provide an example.
7. Begin suspected-misuse review with questions, not accusation.
8. Require human findings and an appeal route before consequences.
9. Review whether unclear directions or inaccessible workflows contributed to the event.
