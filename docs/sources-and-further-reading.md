# Sources and further reading

Last reviewed: **2026-08-10**

These are primary government and standards resources that can inform local review. Their inclusion does not turn this toolkit into legal advice or establish that any particular implementation complies with law.

## U.S. Department of Education: FERPA and student privacy

- Protecting Student Privacy guidance hub: https://studentprivacy.ed.gov/guidance
- Who is a school official under FERPA?: https://studentprivacy.ed.gov/faq/who-school-official-under-ferpa
- Access limited to legitimate educational interests: https://studentprivacy.ed.gov/faq/what-must-educational-agencies-or-institutions-do-ensure-only-school-officials-legitimate
- Responsibilities of third-party service providers under FERPA: https://studentprivacy.ed.gov/resources/responsibilities-third-party-service-providers-under-ferpa
- FERPA regulations, 34 CFR Part 99: https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-34/subtitle-A/part-99

Districts should analyze the specific disclosure pathway, direct-control relationship, permitted purpose, redisclosure limits, legitimate educational interest, annual notice language, contract terms, and applicable state law rather than treating "FERPA compliant" as a generic product label. A vendor's third-party or subprocessor chain can matter to that analysis when education-record information is disclosed beyond the primary vendor.

## Federal Trade Commission: COPPA

- Children's Privacy business guidance: https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/privacy-security/childrens-privacy
- Complying with COPPA, frequently asked questions: https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/complying-coppa-frequently-asked-questions
- COPPA six-step compliance plan: https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/childrens-online-privacy-protection-rule-six-step-compliance-plan-your-business
- COPPA Rule, 16 CFR Part 312: https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-312

FTC guidance includes a section on schools and explains that school consent is limited to the educational context and use for the school's benefit, not unrelated commercial purposes.

## NIST AI risk management

- NIST AI Risk Management Framework: https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework
- NIST AI RMF Core: https://airc.nist.gov/airmf-resources/airmf/5-sec-core/
- NIST AI RMF Playbook: https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework/nist-ai-rmf-playbook
- NIST Generative AI Profile: https://www.nist.gov/publications/artificial-intelligence-risk-management-framework-generative-artificial-intelligence
- NIST AI Resource Center: https://airc.nist.gov/

The AI RMF is voluntary. Its core functions are commonly summarized as govern, map, measure, and manage. The Core explicitly includes policies for risks arising from third-party software, data, and other supply-chain issues, plus contingency processes for failures or incidents involving third-party data or AI systems. Districts can use that structure as a risk-management input without presenting it as a K-12 legal or compliance standard.

NIST states that AI RMF 1.0 is being revised. When relying on a specific NIST practice or citation, verify the current version rather than assuming an older framework text is unchanged.

## Accessibility and conformance review

Accessibility claims should be checked against the actual student, teacher, family, and administrator workflows a district intends to use. A vendor accessibility statement or conformance report can be evidence, but it does not replace district testing, accommodations, or local accessibility review.

## Vendor transparency and subprocessors

A district can preserve a dated vendor subprocessor list as part of its procurement baseline and compare future versions when providers, purposes, data categories, regions, retention terms, or model providers change.

HonorlyAI publishes its current list here: https://honorlyai.com/subprocessors

That link is included as an example of public vendor transparency, not as a claim that the listed architecture automatically satisfies any district's legal, privacy, security, or procurement requirements.

## Local review still matters

Federal sources are not the whole rulebook. Districts should identify applicable state student privacy, biometric, consumer protection, records retention, public records, accessibility, procurement, labor, cybersecurity, and education requirements, along with board policy and collective-bargaining obligations.
