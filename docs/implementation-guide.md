# Implementing K-12 AI governance without creating a shelf document

A school AI policy is useful only when people can make ordinary decisions with it. The goal is not to produce the longest document. The goal is to connect approval, classroom directions, student privacy, support, evidence, and accountability.

## 1. Name an owner and decision group

Identify the person accountable for the implementation and the group that can approve or stop it. Include the roles needed to evaluate curriculum, technology, privacy, legal obligations, accessibility, security, student support, communications, and classroom practice.

Document:

- executive sponsor;
- implementation owner;
- privacy and legal reviewers;
- technology and security reviewers;
- curriculum and instruction leads;
- accessibility and special-services reviewers;
- participating educators;
- family and student feedback channels;
- incident escalation contacts.

## 2. Define one educational purpose

Start with a narrow sentence:

> We are considering `[TOOL OR WORKFLOW]` for `[GROUP]` to support `[SPECIFIC LEARNING OR OPERATIONAL PURPOSE]` during `[TIME PERIOD]`.

Avoid pilots whose purpose is merely "use AI." A test needs an observable problem, intended users, boundaries, and a decision at the end.

## 3. Complete vendor and data review before student use

Use the [vendor evaluation checklist](../templates/district-ai-vendor-evaluation-checklist.md), [privacy review checklist](../templates/school-ai-privacy-review-checklist.md), and [data lifecycle worksheet](../templates/ai-data-lifecycle-worksheet.md).

Resolve, in writing:

- approved users and ages;
- account creation and identity;
- data categories and purposes;
- access and role permissions;
- model training and product-improvement terms;
- subprocessors and data location;
- retention, deletion, export, and contract exit;
- incident notification;
- accessibility and alternative access;
- support and change management.

## 4. Translate district policy into assignment directions

A district policy cannot predict every classroom task. Teachers need a compact way to state what assistance is allowed for each assignment.

Use the [classroom AI use levels](../templates/classroom-ai-use-levels.md), then add assignment-specific examples. A student should not have to guess whether brainstorming, feedback, rewriting, coding help, translation, or final-answer generation is permitted.

## 5. Communicate before launch

Publish plain-language information for students, families, and staff. At minimum, explain:

- what is being used and why;
- who participates;
- what students may and may not do;
- what information is processed;
- what educators can review;
- how to ask questions or report a concern;
- what alternative exists when participation is not appropriate;
- when the district will reevaluate the decision.

The [parent and family AI notice](../templates/parent-family-ai-notice.md) is a starting point, not a substitute for district-specific notice obligations.

## 6. Train for decisions, not button clicks

Product navigation matters, but governance training should focus on judgment:

- selecting an AI use level;
- recognizing when AI help replaces the assessed skill;
- responding to inaccurate or unsafe output;
- escalating privacy and access concerns;
- documenting meaningful interventions;
- supporting students who need alternatives or accommodations;
- avoiding overreliance on automated flags or detectors.

## 7. Run a bounded pilot

Use the [school district AI pilot plan](../templates/school-district-ai-pilot-plan.md). Define:

- start and end dates;
- participating schools, grades, subjects, and staff;
- training and support owners;
- allowed use cases;
- excluded use cases;
- evidence collected;
- stop conditions;
- the date and forum for the expansion decision.

## 8. Measure more than usage

Logins and message counts show activity, not educational value. Consider evidence about:

- teacher time and workload;
- student understanding and independence;
- quality of questions and revision;
- accessibility and participation;
- policy clarity;
- support burden;
- privacy or safety events;
- differences across grade, subject, and learner needs;
- whether the tool changed the assessed work.

Do not collect additional student data merely because a dashboard can display it.

## 9. Publish the decision and change history

At the end of the pilot, record one of four decisions:

1. stop;
2. revise and retest;
3. continue at the same scope;
4. expand with stated conditions.

Publish the evidence considered, material limitations, policy changes, owner, approval date, next review date, and known open questions.

## 10. Treat governance as a loop

Reopen review after a material product change, new data use, new subprocessor, security or privacy incident, expansion to younger students, expansion to a new educational purpose, accessibility concern, or legal and regulatory update.

The policy should be alive enough to learn and stable enough to govern.
