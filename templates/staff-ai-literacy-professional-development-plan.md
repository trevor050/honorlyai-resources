# Staff AI literacy and professional development plan for K-12 districts

> **Canonical HonorlyAI resources:** https://honorlyai.com/resources
>
> **Related guides:** https://honorlyai.com/ai-policy-for-teachers · https://honorlyai.com/ai-use-levels-for-schools · https://honorlyai.com/accessibility
>
> **Direct answer:** A district AI rollout needs more than a policy and a vendor approval. Staff need role-specific practice deciding what AI may do, what humans must still decide, how student data should be handled, how assignment rules are communicated, and what to do when a system is wrong, unsafe, inaccessible, or outside approved use.

Use this template to build a district-owned AI literacy and professional development program. It is vendor-neutral and can be adapted for teachers, school leaders, technology staff, instructional coaches, counselors, special-services teams, librarians, paraprofessionals, and other employees who may encounter generative AI in school workflows.

This resource is a planning aid, not legal advice, a compliance certification, or a substitute for district-specific legal, labor, curriculum, accessibility, privacy, security, and policy review.

## 2. Define AI literacy as observable practice

Avoid a training goal such as "understand AI." That is too vague to evaluate.

By the end of the required foundation, staff should be able to:

- explain in plain language that generative AI can produce plausible but incorrect output;
- distinguish an approved school workflow from a personal or unreviewed AI account;
- identify information that should not be entered into an unapproved tool;
- explain when a human must review, verify, override, or make the final decision;
- state the district's assignment-level AI expectations or locate them quickly;
- recognize that AI-detection output is not the same thing as proof of misconduct;
- describe how accessibility, disability, language, and device constraints can change the quality of an AI workflow;
- identify where to report a privacy, safety, security, access, or content concern;
- document or disclose AI use when district or assignment rules require it;
- distinguish administrative convenience from decisions that affect student rights, placement, discipline, grades, services, or access.

## 3. Role-based training matrix

Do not give every employee the same 90-minute slideshow. Train people for the decisions they actually make.

| Audience | Required foundation | Additional practice | Evidence of readiness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Classroom teachers | AI limits, approved tools, privacy, assignment rules, verification, reporting | assignment design, feedback, tutoring boundaries, disclosure | complete scenario review + publish one sample AI-use statement |
| Administrators | foundation + human judgment + incident escalation | discipline, complaints, family questions, schoolwide consistency | resolve governance scenarios with documented rationale |
| IT / privacy / security | foundation | identity, data flow, retention, subprocessors, logs, incidents, change control | complete vendor/data-flow review exercise |
| Curriculum / instructional coaches | foundation | task design, assessment validity, staff coaching, subject-specific examples | review and revise sample assignments |
| Special services / accessibility | foundation | assistive technology, accommodations, generated-content accessibility, alternative access | accessibility workflow review |
| Counselors / student support | foundation | sensitive data, safety escalation, boundaries on automated judgment | scenario review focused on human escalation |
| Librarians / media specialists | foundation | source verification, citation, research literacy, student coaching | misinformation/source-evaluation exercise |
| Paraprofessionals / support staff | concise foundation | approved use, privacy, student assistance boundaries, escalation | short scenario check |
| Procurement / business office | concise foundation | contracts, evidence requests, renewal, pricing, exit, change notices | procurement question-bank exercise |
| Board / executive leadership | governance briefing | policy ownership, evidence, risk, public communication, review cadence | briefing acknowledgment / discussion record |

Add or remove roles based on local operations.

## 4. Required foundation module

Recommended format: 45 to 75 minutes with live examples and scenarios rather than a lecture-only session.

### Module A: What generative AI can and cannot tell you

Cover:

- generation versus retrieval;
- confident errors and fabricated details;
- stale or incomplete knowledge;
- variability across prompts and repeated runs;
- why verification changes by subject and task;
- why an AI response should not be treated as an authoritative record merely because it is fluent.

**Practice:** Give staff one polished but flawed response and ask them to mark what requires verification.

### Module B: Approved tools and data boundaries

Cover:

- the district's approved AI tools and accounts;
- which workflows are approved for students and which are staff-only;
- categories of student, employee, confidential, or operational information that require special handling;
- the rule for personal accounts and consumer tools;
- where vendor privacy, DPA, retention, and subprocessor information can be found;
- what to do when a useful-looking tool has not been reviewed.

**Practice:** Sort sample information into "approved workflow," "needs review," and "do not enter here" buckets using actual district rules.

Do not invent universal data categories. The district's adopted policy, contracts, applicable law, and counsel determine the final boundaries.

### Module C: Human judgment and consequential decisions

Create a visible line between AI assistance and decisions that require accountable human judgment.

Staff should know that the district does not delegate final judgment to generative AI merely because the tool can produce a recommendation.

Review local rules for uses involving:

- grades and assessment;
- discipline or misconduct allegations;
- special-education or accommodation decisions;
- student risk or safety decisions;
- placement, intervention, eligibility, or service decisions;
- personnel decisions;
- communications that create legal or policy commitments.

**Practice:** For each scenario, identify what AI may assist with and who owns the final decision.

### Module D: Classroom rules and academic integrity

Cover:

- the district's AI use levels or equivalent assignment language;
- why teachers should say what is allowed instead of relying on "use AI responsibly";
- student disclosure expectations;
- preserving the skill being assessed;
- process evidence such as drafts, notes, selected prompts, oral explanation, or version history when proportionate;
- why detector scores or stylistic suspicion should trigger review, not automatic guilt.

**Practice:** Rewrite a vague assignment into one with explicit allowed uses, prohibited uses, disclosure, and evidence.

### Module E: Accessibility, language, and equitable access

Cover:

- keyboard and screen-reader access;
- generated charts, images, math, audio, and other model output that may require accessible alternatives;
- reading level and cognitive load;
- multilingual support versus actual instructional quality;
- speech recognition and accent/language variation;
- low-end devices and bandwidth;
- accommodations and formal plans remaining under educator and district authority.

**Practice:** Test one representative task using at least two access conditions relevant to the district.

### Module F: Safety, security, and incident reporting

Cover the district's actual escalation routes for:

- accidental disclosure of protected or confidential information;
- inappropriate or unsafe generated content;
- unauthorized account access;
- harmful or discriminatory output;
- a broken accessibility workflow;
- suspected prompt injection or malicious content in uploaded material;
- system behavior that conflicts with district policy;
- a vendor or model change that materially changes the approved workflow.

**Practice:** Staff should be able to answer: "Who do I contact, what do I preserve, and what should I stop doing while the issue is reviewed?"

## 5. Teacher practice lab

The foundation should be followed by subject-appropriate practice for educators who will use AI with students.

Each teacher should complete at least one cycle:

1. Select a real upcoming assignment.
2. State the skill or understanding being assessed.
3. Identify which AI help would support that skill.
4. Identify which AI help would replace the skill.
5. Select the district's AI-use level or equivalent boundary.
6. Write student-facing directions.
7. Decide whether disclosure or process evidence is needed.
8. Test the task in the approved tool.
9. Check for likely accessibility and language barriers.
10. Decide what the teacher will review during and after the task.

## 6. Administrator scenario lab

School leaders need practice resolving ambiguity, not merely receiving the teacher module.

Use scenarios such as:

### Scenario 1: Detector score

A teacher receives a high AI-detection score on a student's essay and asks for an automatic zero.

Ask:

- What evidence exists besides the score?
- What policy and assignment directions applied?
- What opportunity does the student have to explain process?
- Who makes the final determination?
- What should be documented?

Related tool: [AI detector and academic-integrity due-process checklist](ai-detector-due-process-checklist.md).

### Scenario 2: Personal AI account

A staff member has been pasting student work containing identifiable information into a personal consumer AI account because the output is useful.

Ask:

- What should stop immediately?
- What facts need to be preserved?
- Which privacy/security owner is notified?
- Does the event meet the district's incident-review threshold?
- What approved alternative should staff use?

Related tool: [AI incident response plan](ai-incident-response-plan.md).

### Scenario 3: AI-generated intervention recommendation

An AI system suggests that a student needs a particular intervention or service.

Ask:

- What evidence produced the suggestion?
- Is the system approved for this purpose?
- Which educator or team owns the decision?
- What other evidence must be considered?
- Could disability, language, incomplete data, or context distort the result?

The model output may inform review only within the district's approved workflow. It does not independently determine eligibility, placement, services, discipline, or other consequential outcomes.

## 7. Technology and privacy tabletop

At least once per year, run a cross-functional exercise with IT, privacy, security, curriculum, and administration.

Choose one realistic change:

- the vendor adds a new model provider;
- retention terms change;
- a new integration gains access to classroom content;
- a teacher requests a popular unapproved tool;
- the vendor adds analytics or a support subprocessor;
- an AI feature begins processing a new data category;
- an outage forces a fallback workflow;
- a feature that passed desktop testing fails on district Chromebooks.

## 8. Training evidence that is actually useful

Do not treat attendance alone as proof of readiness.

Track only what the district needs to operate and improve the program.

Possible evidence:

- completion of required foundation;
- role-specific scenario result;
- teacher-created assignment statement;
- common misconception data from exit questions;
- unresolved questions that require policy clarification;
- support-ticket categories after rollout;
- repeated privacy or access mistakes;
- accessibility issues found during practice;
- staff confidence on specific tasks before and after training.

Avoid collecting unnecessary employee data or using training telemetry for purposes staff were not told about.

### Suggested exit check

Staff should be able to answer all five:

1. Where do I find the district's approved AI tools and rules?
2. What information should I avoid putting into an unapproved tool?
3. When must a human make or verify the final decision?
4. How do I tell students what AI use is allowed on an assignment?
5. Where do I report a privacy, safety, security, accessibility, or policy concern?

If staff cannot answer those questions, the program is not finished because the attendance sheet says 100%.

## 10. Refresh triggers

Do not wait for the annual PD calendar when the operating environment changes.

Review or refresh training when:

- district AI policy changes;
- a new tool or major capability is approved;
- a vendor changes important data handling or subprocessors;
- a material privacy, safety, security, or accessibility incident occurs;
- the district changes assignment-level AI rules;
- a new state or federal requirement materially affects the workflow;
- pilot evidence shows recurring staff confusion;
- families or students repeatedly raise the same unresolved question;
- a feature moves from optional experimentation into routine instructional use.

## 11. 30-60-90 day rollout

### First 30 days

- identify program owner and cross-functional reviewers;
- publish approved-tool and escalation references;
- train pilot staff and administrators;
- collect the first real assignment examples;
- capture misconceptions and unresolved policy questions.

### Days 31-60

- revise training from actual classroom evidence;
- run teacher practice labs;
- hold administrator and technology table-tops;
- publish short FAQs for recurring questions;
- confirm accessibility and language-access support routes.

### Days 61-90

- compare incidents, support requests, and staff questions against the baseline;
- identify departments needing deeper role-specific support;
- retire confusing or obsolete training language;
- document policy questions for district leadership;
- set the next review date and owner.

## 12. District sign-off checklist

Before calling the program ready:

- [ ] Required audiences are identified by role.
- [ ] Staff can locate approved tools and district AI rules.
- [ ] Data-handling examples use district-specific policy rather than generic internet advice.
- [ ] Human-decision boundaries are explicit.
- [ ] Teacher training includes real assignment design practice.
- [ ] Administrators practice misconduct and consequential-decision scenarios.
- [ ] IT/privacy/security staff practice a vendor or data-flow change scenario.
- [ ] Accessibility and multilingual workflows are included.
- [ ] Reporting and escalation routes are concrete.
- [ ] Training includes evidence of understanding beyond attendance.
- [ ] Office hours or a support route exists after launch.
- [ ] Refresh triggers and an accountable owner are documented.
- [ ] The district has reviewed the program with appropriate legal, labor, privacy, accessibility, curriculum, technology, and policy stakeholders.
