> **Canonical guide:** https://honorlyai.com/ai-academic-integrity-policy
>
> **Direct answer:** Academic integrity in the age of AI depends on clear assignment boundaries, student disclosure, evidence of reasoning, proportionate human review, and assessment design. Detection alone is not a policy.

## 1. Purpose

This policy defines how students and educators should use artificial intelligence while preserving honest representation of student work, appropriate attribution, learning, fairness, and due process.

## 2. Core expectations

Students must:

- follow the teacher's stated AI use level and directions;
- represent their own knowledge, reasoning, and contribution honestly;
- disclose AI assistance when required;
- verify claims, calculations, quotations, sources, and citations;
- preserve process evidence when the assignment requires it;
- be able to explain submitted work;
- avoid fabricating evidence, experiences, observations, interviews, citations, data, or authorship.

Educators must:

- state AI expectations before the work is due;
- distinguish approved assistance from prohibited substitution;
- make accessibility tools and approved accommodations clear;
- use proportionate evidence and human review;
- avoid treating AI-detection output as conclusive proof;
- apply policy consistently while considering age, instruction, intent, and context.

## 3. Assignment-level rules

Every relevant assignment should state:

- the AI use level;
- approved tools and accounts;
- allowed and prohibited uses;
- disclosure requirements;
- process evidence, if any;
- consequences or revision opportunities;
- who to ask when uncertain.

Use the [classroom AI use levels](classroom-ai-use-levels.md).

## 4. Examples of acceptable use

When authorized, acceptable use may include:

- practice questions or explanations;
- brainstorming before independent selection and development;
- feedback on student-created work;
- debugging student-written code;
- critique against a rubric;
- translation or accessibility support within approved boundaries;
- comparison and correction of AI output;
- AI-integrated tasks that assess verification, revision, and judgment.

## 5. Examples of violations

Depending on assignment directions, violations may include:

- submitting generated work as original student work;
- using AI during a Level 1 task;
- exceeding the allowed stage or type of assistance;
- fabricating citations, data, interviews, or observations;
- hiding required AI use;
- using another person's account, prompts, files, or work without authorization;
- bypassing safeguards or access controls;
- using AI to impersonate, harass, deceive, or invade privacy;
- sharing restricted assessment material with a public service.

## 6. Disclosure

When disclosure is required, students should provide enough information to understand the role AI played without producing unnecessary personal data.

Suggested statement:

> I used `[TOOL]` during `[STAGE]` to `[PURPOSE]`. I verified, rejected, or revised the output by `[METHOD]`. The final reasoning and submission are my responsibility.

Teachers may request selected prompts, outputs, version history, notes, annotations, or an oral explanation when proportionate to the assignment.

## 7. Investigation and evidence

A concern may begin with unusual work, inconsistent process evidence, a disclosure issue, a system log, or an automated flag. No single signal should automatically determine misconduct.

The reviewer should:

1. confirm that the assignment rule was clear and available;
2. identify the specific suspected violation;
3. preserve only relevant evidence;
4. give the student an opportunity to explain;
5. consider drafts, notes, source use, version history, oral explanation, and prior demonstrated skill;
6. consider accessibility, language, and approved support;
7. distinguish factual findings from probabilities or system guesses;
8. document the human decision and available appeal.

## 8. Responses

Responses should be educational, proportionate, and consistent with existing policy. Options may include:

- clarification and reteaching;
- corrected disclosure;
- revision or reassessment;
- a process reflection;
- temporary restriction of an AI feature;
- family communication;
- consequences under existing academic integrity or conduct policy;
- support referral when the behavior reflects unmet learning or access needs.

## 9. Assessment design

The district should help educators design assessments that gather evidence AI cannot silently replace, including:

- checkpoints and drafts;
- oral explanation;
- live or in-class performance;
- local or personal application that does not require disclosure of sensitive information;
- comparison and critique;
- transfer to a new task;
- process annotation;
- iterative feedback and revision.

The goal is not to make every assignment AI-proof. It is to make the assessed learning visible.

## 10. Appeals and review

Students should receive:

- notice of the concern;
- the specific rule allegedly violated;
- an opportunity to respond;
- a human decision;
- an appeal or review route consistent with district policy.
