# Teacher classroom AI guidelines

> **Canonical guide:** https://honorlyai.com/ai-policy-for-teachers
>
> These guidelines translate district AI governance into repeatable classroom decisions. Adapt them to local policy, grade level, subject, assessment design, accessibility, and approved tools.

## 1. Start with the learning target

Before allowing or assigning AI, write down:

- what students should know or be able to do;
- what evidence will demonstrate that learning;
- which parts require independent performance;
- which AI assistance would support learning;
- which AI assistance would replace the skill being assessed.

Do not select a tool first and invent the learning purpose afterward.

## 2. State the AI use level

Put one of the district's [classroom AI use levels](classroom-ai-use-levels.md) on the assignment. Add concrete examples for the task.

Assignment directions should answer:

- Is AI allowed?
- Which approved tool or account may be used?
- During which stages may it be used?
- What may it generate or change?
- What is prohibited?
- What process evidence or disclosure is required?
- What should students do when they are uncertain?

## 3. Use school-approved accounts and workflows

- Do not direct students to create personal accounts unless the district has expressly approved that workflow.
- Do not ask students to enter information outside the approved data scope.
- Use district-provided integrations and settings.
- Confirm that the tool and workflow are approved for the students' age, grade, and use case.
- Provide an accessible alternative when needed.

## 4. Keep student thinking visible

AI may support learning through:

- questions and hints;
- worked examples that do not duplicate the assessed answer;
- practice and retrieval;
- feedback on student-created work;
- comparison of approaches;
- debugging and error analysis;
- revision suggestions;
- reflection on model limitations.

Use checkpoints that let students explain choices, show drafts, annotate changes, defend claims, or transfer the skill to a new problem.

## 5. Teach verification

Students should learn that fluent output is not necessarily accurate, fair, safe, or well-sourced.

Model how to:

- verify factual claims against reliable sources;
- rerun calculations independently;
- locate and inspect original sources;
- reject fabricated citations;
- identify missing context and assumptions;
- compare outputs across prompts or methods;
- preserve uncertainty instead of inventing certainty;
- recognize when a question needs a qualified professional or trusted adult.

## 6. Treat automated flags as prompts for review

A model-generated warning, integrity flag, safety classification, or AI-detection score is not proof by itself.

When reviewing a concern:

1. inspect the assignment directions;
2. gather relevant process evidence;
3. ask the student to explain the work;
4. consider accessibility, language, and support needs;
5. distinguish misunderstanding from deliberate circumvention;
6. follow district policy and document the human decision.

## 7. Protect privacy

Do not enter or ask students to enter unauthorized personal, confidential, health, discipline, disability, family, or identifying information.

Avoid pasting entire student records or peer work when a smaller excerpt or fictionalized example will do. Use the minimum information necessary for the instructional purpose.

Report suspected unauthorized access, disclosure, or unexpected personal information through `[DISTRICT REPORTING ROUTE]`.

## 8. Review sensitive or high-impact uses

Do not use AI as the sole or final decision-maker for grades, discipline, special-education decisions, mental-health decisions, threat assessments, admissions, placement, or other high-impact actions.

Before any approved AI assistance in these areas, follow district procedures for professional judgment, documentation, review, notice, and appeal.

## 9. Plan for inaccurate or harmful output

Tell students what to do when AI produces:

- dangerous instructions;
- sexual or violent content;
- hateful or discriminatory output;
- self-harm or crisis content;
- personal information;
- harassment or impersonation;
- confident misinformation;
- output that appears to exploit or manipulate the student.

The classroom response should prioritize immediate safety and human support. Do not require a student to keep interacting with harmful content merely to document it.

## 10. Reflect after the assignment

Ask:

- Did AI improve understanding or merely polish output?
- Could students explain and transfer the learning?
- Were directions clear?
- Did the workflow create unequal access or extra burden?
- Did teacher visibility provide useful context without unnecessary surveillance?
- Which errors or misconceptions were common?
- Should the AI use level change next time?
