# AI detector and academic-integrity due-process checklist

> **Purpose:** A review worksheet for suspected AI-assisted misconduct that prevents automated detector scores or vague bans from becoming automatic findings.
>
> **Canonical guide:** https://honorlyai.com/ai-academic-integrity-policy

## 1. Confirm that the rule existed before the work began

- [ ] The assignment included a visible AI-use statement.
- [ ] The statement identified allowed actions.
- [ ] The statement identified prohibited actions.
- [ ] Approved tools, accounts, and data limits were identified.
- [ ] Disclosure requirements were stated.
- [ ] Students received the direction before beginning the assignment.
- [ ] The direction addressed relevant embedded features such as translation, grammar rewriting, autocomplete, code completion, or image generation.
- [ ] Approved accommodations and assistive technology were not accidentally prohibited.

If the rule was absent, contradictory, inaccessible, or not taught, document that before considering an individual misconduct finding.

## 2. Record the concern without converting it into a verdict

Describe the observable reason for review:

`[INSERT FACTUAL DESCRIPTION]`

Examples of observable concerns may include:

- a major unexplained change from in-class work;
- citations that do not exist or do not support the claim;
- inability to explain central reasoning;
- version history inconsistent with the stated process;
- prohibited AI records from a school-governed system;
- direct admission or reliable witness evidence;
- an automated detector result that prompts further inquiry.

Avoid statements such as “the detector proved cheating” or “the writing sounds like AI.”

Confirm:

- [ ] The score is treated as an uncertain signal, not proof.
- [ ] The reviewer understands that the tool did not observe authorship.
- [ ] The tool cannot determine whether any assistance violated the assignment rule.
- [ ] The reviewer considered false positives, mixed human-AI work, paraphrasing, translation, editing, and subgroup performance limitations.
- [ ] The student is not required to prove innocence against an unexplained score.

## 4. Gather independent process evidence

Review only evidence relevant to the assignment and policy.

- [ ] assignment directions and rubric;
- [ ] student's notes or outline;
- [ ] drafts and version history;
- [ ] source annotations and retrieved sources;
- [ ] calculations, test cases, or intermediate work;
- [ ] in-class work using comparable skills;
- [ ] permitted AI disclosure or school-governed AI records;
- [ ] teacher feedback and revision history;
- [ ] student's explanation of key decisions;
- [ ] a short oral, in-class, or transfer task where appropriate;
- [ ] accommodation and language-support documentation relevant to the work.

Do not demand private-account passwords, unrelated personal-device history, or access outside district authority and policy.

## 5. Invite the student's explanation

Use neutral questions before presenting conclusions.

Suggested questions:

1. Walk me through how you began the assignment.
2. Which part was most difficult?
3. How did you choose this claim, method, source, structure, or design?
4. Which tools or people helped you?
5. What changed between your first and final versions?
6. Can you explain this sentence, step, citation, or code section?
7. Did you use AI in any way? What did it do, and what did you do?
8. Is there an accommodation, translation tool, tutoring support, or other context the review should consider?
9. What evidence of your process can you provide?

Record the student's explanation accurately and provide language or disability access needed for meaningful participation.

## 6. Review accommodations, language, and bias risk

- [ ] The reviewer checked whether assistive technology or translation could explain the observed difference.
- [ ] The student's writing development, editing support, and language background were considered without stereotyping.
- [ ] The review did not assume that polished, formulaic, concise, or error-free writing is inherently machine-generated.
- [ ] The district considered whether the detector or alert has documented performance differences across language or student groups.
- [ ] A qualified staff member reviewed relevant accommodation questions.

## 8. Select a proportionate response

Possible outcomes include:

- no violation supported;
- unclear rule or insufficient evidence;
- instructional conference;
- opportunity to redo or verify the work;
- correction of missing disclosure;
- academic consequence under the adopted policy;
- referral under existing school procedure;
- change to the assignment direction or tool configuration;
- staff training or policy revision.

## 9. Provide notice and appeal

The student and family, when applicable, should receive:

- [ ] the assignment rule at issue;
- [ ] the evidence considered;
- [ ] the limitations of any automated tool used;
- [ ] the student's recorded explanation;
- [ ] the human findings;
- [ ] the consequence or corrective action;
- [ ] the review or appeal route;
- [ ] the deadline and contact for appeal.

## 10. Review the system, not only the student

After the case, ask:

- Was the assignment direction clear enough?
- Did the approved tool make the boundary visible?
- Did staff over-rely on an automated score or alert?
- Did an accommodation, language need, or access issue contribute?
- Does the policy distinguish accidental, unclear, and intentional misuse?
- Should this assignment collect better process evidence next time?
- Should the district change training, configuration, or approved-tool guidance?
